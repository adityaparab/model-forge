# CECL: ASC Topic 326, Current Expected Credit Losses

## What the standard is

The current expected credit loss model, usually called CECL, is the US GAAP framework for measuring credit loss allowances. The Financial Accounting Standards Board introduced it through Accounting Standards Update 2016-13, issued in June 2016, which created Topic 326 of the Accounting Standards Codification. Subtopic 326-20 covers financial assets measured at amortised cost, and subtopic 326-30 covers credit losses on available-for-sale debt securities.

CECL replaced the earlier incurred loss approach, under which a loss was recognised only once it was probable that it had been incurred. Under CECL there is no probability threshold for recognition: an entity records an allowance for the credit losses it expects over the full contractual life of an asset from the time the asset is originated or acquired.

## Scope

Subtopic 326-20 applies to financial assets measured at amortised cost, which for a lender typically includes loans held for investment and held-to-maturity debt securities, as well as net investments in leases and off-balance-sheet credit exposures such as unfunded loan commitments that are not unconditionally cancellable. Available-for-sale debt securities follow a separate approach under subtopic 326-30.

## Measurement principles

The allowance reflects lifetime expected credit losses. Estimates are based on relevant information about past events, current conditions, and reasonable and supportable forecasts of future conditions. For periods beyond the horizon over which an entity can make reasonable and supportable forecasts, the entity reverts to historical loss information.

Assets that share similar risk characteristics are evaluated collectively in pools; assets that do not share such characteristics are evaluated individually. The standard does not prescribe a single estimation method. Approaches seen in practice include loss-rate methods, vintage analysis, roll-rate methods, discounted cash flow methods, and methods built from probability of default and loss given default.

## Effective dates

The standard became effective first for SEC filers that were not smaller reporting companies, for fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2019. A later amendment deferred the effective date for other entities, including smaller reporting companies and private entities, to fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2022.

## Relevance to a PD model

A probability-of-default model can be one input to a CECL estimate when an institution chooses a PD and LGD method. Several differences between a scorecard PD and a CECL input are worth noting in a development document:

- **Horizon.** A scorecard is usually calibrated to a fixed outcome window, often twelve months. CECL needs losses over the contractual life, so a one-period PD must be extended into a term structure of default probabilities across the remaining life.
- **Conditioning.** CECL estimates reflect current conditions and reasonable and supportable forecasts. A scorecard calibrated to an average historical default rate would need adjustment, often through macroeconomic overlays or conditioning, before it reflects forecast conditions.
- **Pooling.** CECL evaluates assets with similar risk characteristics together, and score bands or risk grades from a PD model are one common way to form such pools.

## Use in this corpus

This summary gives the documentation agent context on how a PD model's outputs relate to the US accounting framework for credit losses. It describes the standard only. It makes no claim that any model or allowance process meets the standard, and a scorecard built for credit decisioning would need further work before it could support an allowance estimate.
