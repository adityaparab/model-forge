# IFRS 9: Expected Credit Loss Impairment

## What the standard is

IFRS 9 Financial Instruments is the International Financial Reporting Standard that covers classification and measurement, impairment, and hedge accounting for financial instruments. The International Accounting Standards Board published the complete version in July 2014, and it became mandatory for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2018, replacing IAS 39. This summary covers only the impairment requirements, which use an expected credit loss model.

Like the US CECL standard, IFRS 9 moved away from an incurred loss model so that allowances reflect credit losses expected in the future rather than waiting for a loss event. Unlike CECL, IFRS 9 does not require lifetime losses for every asset from origination; it uses a staged approach.

## The three-stage approach

- **Stage 1.** For instruments whose credit risk has not increased significantly since initial recognition, the allowance equals the expected credit losses that result from default events possible within the next twelve months.
- **Stage 2.** When credit risk has increased significantly since initial recognition, the allowance moves to lifetime expected credit losses, although the asset is not yet credit-impaired.
- **Stage 3.** For credit-impaired assets, the allowance remains at lifetime expected credit losses, and interest revenue is calculated on the carrying amount net of the allowance.

Movement between stages is symmetric: if the significant increase in credit risk reverses, an instrument can return to the twelve-month measure.

## Significant increase in credit risk

The assessment compares the risk of default over the expected life at the reporting date with the risk at initial recognition. The standard sets a rebuttable presumption that credit risk has increased significantly when contractual payments are more than 30 days past due. It also allows a simplification for instruments judged to have low credit risk at the reporting date. Entities are expected to use reasonable and supportable forward-looking information where it is available without undue cost or effort, rather than relying only on past-due status.

## Measuring expected credit losses

Expected credit losses are an unbiased, probability-weighted amount determined by evaluating a range of possible outcomes, reflecting the time value of money, and using reasonable and supportable information about past events, current conditions and forecasts of future economic conditions. In practice many lenders compute them from probability of default, loss given default and exposure at default, often under several weighted macroeconomic scenarios.

The standard does not define default precisely, but it requires an entity to apply a definition consistent with its internal credit risk management and includes a rebuttable presumption that default does not occur later than 90 days past due.

A simplified approach, with lifetime losses from the start, is required or permitted for certain trade receivables, contract assets and lease receivables.

## Relevance to a PD model

A PD model feeds an IFRS 9 estimate in two ways. First, the stage 1 measure needs a twelve-month PD, and stages 2 and 3 need a lifetime PD term structure. Second, the stage assessment itself often relies on comparing a current PD with the PD at origination. Because IFRS 9 estimates are point-in-time and forward-looking, a scorecard calibrated to an average historical default rate would usually need scenario conditioning before it could serve as an input.
